The project in 30 seconds

A clearer location decision.
A more confident pickup.

I connected the map, address list, and confirmation screens around one focused task: choosing the right store and knowing where to collect an order.

The problem
A small map icon was easy to miss. Similar addresses left users to work out location and distance themselves.
My approach
Inclusive personas shaped the priorities. Paper-prototype testing with three participants exposed what still needed to change.
What I learned
A visible map helped participants compare stores, but confirmation also needed a route to the actual pickup location.

01 / Understand the mismatch

Discover

A wrong-store order led me to question how the app helps people connect an address with a real place.

It started with my own coffee order.

In my first year, I ordered from a Tim Hortons across the street instead of the one on campus. Unfamiliar with the area, I rushed to collect my coffee and arrived late for class.

I initially thought the app had no map. A closer review revealed one behind a small icon. The problem became clearer: spatial information was difficult to discover at the moment of choosing a store.

The original experience

Where the ordering journey breaks down

Research → user needs

Location uncertainty follows the whole order.

I combined user interviews, App Store reviews, and literature research, then used inclusive personas and a journey map to examine how different constraints affected store selection.

Research findingUser needDesign direction

User need Spatial context at the moment of choosing.

Design direction Make nearby stores visible on the map by default.

User need A visual way to compare stores alongside text.

03 / Test the complete task

Develop

Paper prototyping let me test my assumptions about location selection before committing to the final interface.

Usability testing

Three participants.
One complete pickup journey.

Participants used the paper prototype under simulated constraints, including distraction and one-handed interaction. Pickup was simulated in a room with chairs representing stores.

  1. 01

    Find and select the closest store.

  2. 02

  3. 03

Observation → iteration

Two observations changed the design.

01

Showing a map was only the starting point.

Observed: participants still noticed addresses first. The map helped them compare proximity, but my first prototype displayed a store pin only after an address was selected.

Changed: show all nearby pins upfront and connect them with the store cards. Users can begin with the map or the list.

→
02

Confirmation did not yet help people get there.

Observed: participants relied mostly on text after ordering; the confirmation map alone did not help them navigate to the store.

Changed: keep the chosen location visible and add a direct Google Maps link on the order preparation screen.

Evidence boundary: these were qualitative observations from three paper-prototype sessions. Simulated constraints do not represent lived disability experience. The final changes still need follow-up testing.

Learning & potential value

What the evidence supports.
What I would validate next.

Observed in testing
The integrated map helped participants compare locations. The pickup task exposed a need for navigation beyond confirmation.
Potential business value
Clearer choices could reduce wrong-location orders and service friction. A more understandable flow could reduce abandonment and strengthen customer confidence. These benefits have not been measured.
Next validation
Test the final prototype against the original flow. Measure task completion, location-selection time, and unnecessary interactions; evaluate wrong-location orders and abandonment in a live product.

Reflection

A complete task ends at the right store.

Inclusive design helped me see beyond a successful tap. Testing challenged my assumption that selecting a store was enough: people also needed to confirm it and get there.

I would audit existing flows more carefully at the start, involve people with lived accessibility needs, and validate screen-reader labels, map alternatives, and touch targets in the next round.
